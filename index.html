    Why the rules matter

    ~4 min

    Your broker is accountable for everything you do with AI. There is no AI law for this work yet, so the rules come from privacy law and mortgage licensing law.

    Why, and the law behind it
    Not legal advice

    This guide was built from public sources and reviewed by the LendMate team, not by a lawyer. It explains what the rules say; it does not replace the broker’s own compliance advice. LAW means a statute, regulation or regulator guidance, with a source ID that matches research/sources.md. LENDMATE RULE is our house policy. TOOL POLICY is what a vendor publishes about its own product, and it can change at any time.

    Your broker carries the risk

    One: PIPEDA Schedule 1, clause 4.1.3. An organization is responsible for personal information it transfers to a third party for processing, and must use contracts or other means to keep a comparable level of protection (F6). Two: the OPC’s 2009 guidelines on processing across borders. PIPEDA does not prohibit sending data outside Canada, but the sending organization stays accountable and must make it plain that information may be processed in a foreign country and may be accessible to that country’s authorities (F10). Three: the joint federal, provincial and territorial principles on generative AI, Principle 5 on accountability (F5). Takeaway: anything you do with an AI tool is legally the broker’s responsibility, which is why the broker’s rules always win.

    Two kinds of client data

    LendMate works for two layers of clients. Our client is the mortgage broker (B2B). The broker’s clients are borrowers and buyers (B2C). Both layers of data are private, and the VA is the third party handling it. B2C, all LAW: PIPEDA says the organization stays accountable for personal information it sends to a third party for processing (F6), and the regulators say the organization, not the tool, is accountable (F5). Ontario brokerages may not use client information for any other purpose without written consent (C-ON5, O. Reg. 188/08 s.57). BC and Alberta require client confidentiality (C-BC7, C-AB3). BC records stay in BC and under the brokerage’s direct control, Ontario records stay at the brokerage’s premises, Alberta records at its Alberta office. B2B is a LendMate rule, not law: no statute we researched covers a broker’s own business information. The LendMate team confirmed on 2 November 2026 that it is handled exactly like the borrowers’ data. The mistake rule is also a confirmed LendMate rule. The broker, not the VA, decides what has to be reported and to whom; PIPEDA and the BC and Alberta rules have breach and loss reporting duties that sit with the organization or brokerage (F9, C-BC5, P3).

    No AI law yet

    F1, OPC principles for generative AI, issued 7 December 2023, guidance only. F24, LEGISinfo for Bill C-27 (44th Parliament, 1st session): final status “At consideration in committee in the House of Commons”, no Royal Assent, session ended 6 January 2025. AIDA was part of that bill. F22, LEGISinfo for Bill C-36 (45-1): introduced 15 June 2026, status “At second reading in the House of Commons”. It may have moved since 28 October 2026. F20, OSFI Guideline E-23 (2027): effective 1 May 2027, applies to federally regulated financial institutions, and its definition of a model includes AI/ML methods. It does not apply to brokers directly; we found no source on whether lenders pass its requirements down to brokers (F21, not found).

    LAW · F11 · F12 · P2 · C-BC5

    What privacy regulators expect from AI usersFive principles to work by

    1. Only put personal information into a prompt where authorised. For you, “authorised” means your broker has approved it.

    2. Use anonymized or de-identified information where possible.

    3. What the AI infers about a person is personal information too.

    4. Check AI output is accurate, especially when it feeds a decision about a person.

    5. The organization is accountable, never the tool.

    LAW (regulator guidance) · F2 to F5

    Why, and the law behind it
    The five layers

    Layer 1: PIPEDA applies to private-sector organizations across Canada in commercial activity (F11). FINTRAC obligations apply to mortgage brokers, administrators and lenders from 11 October 2024 (F13). CASL sets the rules for commercial electronic messages (F18). Layer 2: Alberta and BC have their own private-sector privacy laws, deemed substantially similar to PIPEDA (F12). Inside those provinces they generally replace PIPEDA; information that crosses provincial or national borders stays under PIPEDA (F11). Layer 3: each province’s mortgage law, covered in section 5. Layer 4: vendor settings. Layer 5: the broker client’s own rules. “Follow the stricter one” is a LendMate house rule, not a legal requirement.

    Which privacy law

    F12: the OPC lists only Alberta, BC and Quebec as having general private-sector privacy laws deemed substantially similar to PIPEDA. Ontario, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador and Nova Scotia have substantially similar laws for health information custodians only, which is not our work. F11: the OPC says organizations in the Northwest Territories, Yukon and Nunavut are considered federally regulated, so PIPEDA covers them, and that businesses handling personal information that crosses provincial or national borders are subject to PIPEDA. Every LendMate file crosses a national border, because the VA is in the Philippines. P2: Alberta PIPA section 13.1 requires the organization to notify the individual, before or at the time of transfer, of how to get its policies on service providers outside Canada and who can answer questions. That is the broker’s notice to give, not the VA’s. C-BC5: BC Mortgage Services Rules s.109(2), immediate written notice to the superintendent if records are lost or improperly accessed. BC PIPA itself has no mandatory breach notification (P1).

    The five principles

    These come from the joint principles for generative AI issued 7 December 2023 by the OPC with the provincial and territorial privacy regulators. The document is guidance on how existing privacy law applies; it is not a statute (F1). 1 (F2, Principle 7): “Where personal information (and, in particular, sensitive or confidential information) must be entered into a prompt, only do so where authorised.” 2 (F2): “Where possible and reasonable, use anonymized or de-identified information within prompts.” 3 (F3): “Treat any inferences generated about an identifiable individual as personal information.” 4 (F4, Principle 8): take reasonable steps to ensure outputs are accurate as necessary for the purpose, especially if used to make or assist in decisions about individuals. 5 (F5, Principle 5): “Accountability for decisions rests with the organization, and not with any kind of automated system.”

    03

    The licensed line

    ~3 min

    LendMate VAs are not mortgage licensed and only do unlicensed tasks. If a task needs a licence, it goes to your broker. Using AI doesn’t change that.

    This is the most important idea in this training, and it is not really about AI. In several provinces, tasks that sound like admin are defined in the law as mortgage brokering. AI tools will happily draft advice, qualification wording or lender submissions when asked, which makes it easy to cross the line without noticing.

    No

    LENDMATE RULE · Built on the licensing laws above

    Why, and the law behind it
    The LendMate position

    Confirmed by the LendMate team on 28 October 2026: LendMate VAs are not mortgage licensed, only do unlicensed activities, and no current VA task sits on the licensed side. Anything on the licensed side is for the licensed broker. Only a licensed person acting for a brokerage may do these tasks for pay.

    Licensed tasks, by province

    Every cell is from the statute or regulator text. Ontario MBLAA s.2(1): soliciting, providing information about a prospective borrower to a prospective lender, assessing a borrower on behalf of a lender, negotiating or arranging (C-ON1); s.2(3): no individual may deal in mortgages for remuneration, “as an employee or otherwise”, without a licence (C-ON2). BC Mortgage Services Act s.1 adds providing advice about entering into a particular mortgage contract (C-BC2); s.4 has no exemption for brokerage staff (C-BC3). Saskatchewan s.2(4): soliciting on behalf of another, negotiating or arranging, advice on the appropriateness of a particular mortgage loan; s.5(3) “as an employee or otherwise” (C-SK1). Manitoba s.1: soliciting, providing information about a prospective borrower to a lender, assessing a borrower for a lender, arranging or placing mortgages (C-MB1); s.2(2) exempts office staff in clerical, accounting or caretaking duties not otherwise in mortgage business (C-MB2). Newfoundland and Labrador s.2(2): soliciting, negotiating or arranging, advice on the appropriateness of a particular mortgage (C-NL1). Alberta Real Estate Act s.1(1)(r): solicits, negotiates, collects payments or administers (C-AB1). New Brunswick: FCNB guidelines say taking mortgage applications or pre-screening referrals requires a licence (C-NB1). Gaps: the Nova Scotia Act definition could not be read (C-NS3). No licensing statute was found for PEI or the territories.

    AI drafts, your broker decides

    This is a LendMate house rule. It applies the licensing definitions to AI output. Recommending a product is advice (BC, SK, NL), saying a borrower qualifies is assessing (ON, BC, MB), sending to a lender is providing borrower information to a lender (ON, BC, MB), negotiating terms is negotiating or arranging (most provinces). The “may draft” column is drafting that a licensed broker reviews and sends. No private information goes into the tool, so these are templates the broker or VA fills in outside AI.

    LENDMATE RULE
    Why, and the law behind it
    The traffic light

    A LendMate house rule, set by the LendMate team on 30 October 2026. The regulators’ principles say to enter personal information into a prompt only where authorised, and to use de-identified information where possible (F2). LendMate goes further on purpose: no private information in chat apps at all. Yellow is the one narrow exception, for tools that handle private information by design. It needs the broker’s own account and the broker’s confirmation that client consent covers it. Consent is never assumed. Green is anything with no private information in it; facts and ad rules still apply.

    Red data

    Row 1, F2 plus LendMate rule: the law says “only where authorised”; the full ban on private information in chat apps is LendMate’s own stricter rule, not law. Row 2, F7: PIPEDA Schedule 1 clause 4.3.4 says some information, for example medical records and income records, is almost always considered to be sensitive. T4s, NOAs, pay stubs and bank statements are income records. Row 3, C-ON5: Ontario O. Reg. 188/08 s.57, a brokerage shall not use information obtained in the course of business for any other purpose without the written consent of the person it is about. Row 4, C-BC7 and C-AB3: BC Mortgage Services Rules s.38(e) and 39(4)(b), maintain client confidentiality and do not disclose confidential client information unless the client authorizes it or the law requires it; RECA Rule 44(1) and 44(2)(a), no disclosure of client information without authorization, and mortgage brokerages must have policies to protect personal and confidential information. Row 5, F15: FINTRAC’s guidance on methods to verify identity. Government photo ID must be authentic, valid and current; a fax, photocopy, scan or electronic image counts only as one source under the dual-process method. An agent may verify identity only under a written agreement with the brokerage, which stays responsible. Row 6, LendMate rule set by the LendMate team on 29 October 2026: any action in a credit bureau or lender portal is done by a human, never by an AI tool. The bureau and portal contract terms are private and were not researched (E1).

    Yellow

    Yellow covers tools that handle private information by design: AI notetakers on client calls and AI features inside GHL or My Broker Pro. Steps 1 and 2 are LendMate house rules. The broker collects client consent, so LendMate cannot confirm it from our side, and consent to a mortgage application may not cover AI. Until a broker confirms, these tools stay off for that broker’s clients. Step 3 is law. OPC guidance on recording calls requires notice and a choice (F17). Alberta PIPA s.13.1 requires notice when a service provider outside Canada handles personal information (P2); that notice is the broker’s to give. Ontario O. Reg. 188/08 s.57 requires written consent to use client information for any other purpose (C-ON5). My Broker Pro’s published terms don’t cover AI, and its pages conflict on where data is hosted (D9).

    Green

    Green means no personal information, not no rules. Row 1, F4: the OPC principles ask organizations to take reasonable steps to make sure AI outputs are accurate enough for their purpose. Row 2, F19: Competition Act s.74.01(1)(a), a representation to the public that is false or misleading in a material respect is reviewable conduct; 74.01(1)(b) covers performance claims not based on an adequate and proper test. Row 3, C-ON6: Ontario O. Reg. 187/08 s.8(1), all public relations materials must clearly and prominently disclose the licensee name and the brokerage’s authorized name and licence number; s.9 bans false, misleading or deceptive information. Row 4, C-AB4: RECA Rule 65(b), the brokerage must ensure its name is clearly indicated in the course of dealing in mortgages. Row 5, F18: CASL s.6, a commercial electronic message needs consent, prescribed sender identification, contact information and an unsubscribe mechanism; electronic messages include text, sound, voice and image messages. Ad rules for other provinces were not researched; ask the broker.

Official sites blocked automated reading and secondary sources disagree, so this means “not found”, not “there is no law”. This has not been confirmed with each government. PIPEDA applies, and organizations in the territories are considered federally regulated (F11). FINTRAC’s mortgage sector duties apply, including keeping records at least five years and producing them within 30 days (F13, F14).     Recording? Confirm with your broker first, then say it at the start of the call.

    Quick answersCan I...?

    The scriptWhat to say

    Say this at the start · LendMate rule“This call is recorded and transcribed so [purpose]. Is that OK? We can also continue without recording.”

    Fill [purpose] with the real reason your broker gives, as narrowly as possible.

    1. Broker confirmed, before or during the call? If not, no notetaker.

    2. Say it’s recorded, and why, as narrowly as you can.

    3. Offer a choice. If recording isn’t essential, continue without it.

    4. One party’s consent makes recording legal under the Criminal Code. Privacy law still needs the notice.

    5. New Brunswick: notes of key client calls must be kept.

    LAW · F16 · F17 · C-NB3

    Why, and the law behind it

    A notetaker only runs on a client call after the broker confirms, before or during the call, that client consent covers it, in the broker’s own account (LendMate rule). The script wording is a LendMate house rule. F17, OPC guidance on recording customer calls (page modified 8 October 2026): the organization must inform the customer that it is recording, state the purpose clearly and narrowly, and in most cases a customer who continues after being told has given implied consent; express consent may be needed for voiceprints or other sensitive information; customers must be given a choice, and where recording isn’t integral to the service, alternatives must be offered; businesses that outsource call handling must make sure the third parties follow the same rules. F16, Criminal Code s.184: intercepting a private communication is an offence, but s.184(2)(a) excludes a person who has the consent, express or implied, of the originator or the intended recipient. C-NB3: New Brunswick brokerages keep notes of communications about client instructions, disclosure, suitability, material risks and complaints.

    Words you’ll see

    ~2 min

    Short meanings for the names and labels used in this guide.

    PIPEDA
    The federal privacy law. It covers private businesses across Canada, and any personal information that crosses a provincial or national border.
    Alberta PIPA and BC PIPA
    The provincial privacy laws in Alberta and BC. Inside those provinces they generally replace PIPEDA.
    OPC
    The Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada. With the provincial and territorial privacy regulators, it published the principles for generative AI.
    FINTRAC
    Anti-money laundering rules. They set how ID is checked and how long records are kept (at least 5 years).
    CASL
    The rules for commercial emails and texts: consent, sender details and an unsubscribe.
    OSFI Guideline E-23
    Rules for AI models at federally regulated lenders, starting 1 May 2027. They do not apply to brokers directly.
    Licensed activity
    A task the law says only a licensed mortgage person may do for pay, such as advising, assessing a borrower or arranging a mortgage.
    Mortgage regulator
    The body that licenses brokers in each province, such as FSRA in Ontario, BCFSA in BC and RECA in Alberta.
    B2C data
    Your broker’s clients’ information: borrowers and buyers.
    B2B data
    Your broker’s own business information: logins, systems, client lists, pipeline, lender details.
    Placeholder
    A blank such as [First name] that you fill in later, outside the AI tool.
    Notetaker
    An AI tool that records and transcribes a call.
    CRM AI
    AI features inside a client system, such as GHL or My Broker Pro.
